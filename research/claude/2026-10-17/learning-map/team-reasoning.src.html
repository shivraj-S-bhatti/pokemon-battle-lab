Why is this team good?

A team is good against some opponents, in some board states, played some way. To reason about that you have to answer four smaller questions, each with its own math, its own data and its own way of going wrong.

What can happen?Mechanics. The engine knows.
What is probably true?Beliefs about hidden sets.
What does this pairing enable?Interactions and plans.
What should I click?Decisions under a guessing game.

The fifth question, "is the team good?", is an average of the fourth over the opponents you expect to meet:

T* = argmaxT  EO ~ metagame [ Pr(win | T, O, π) ]

Read it as: pick the team T whose average win chance is highest, where opponents O are drawn from the metagame and both sides are played by policy π. Change the pilot or the metagame and the best team changes too.

The decomposition

Five layers, bottom to top

Each layer rests on the one below it. Most bad team analysis, by people or by LLMs, comes from answering one layer with tools that belong to another, such as treating a mechanism as if it were a probability.

5 · Team valueIs the team good?
Represent
Matchup matrix: your team vs. each common archetype, win rate per cell.
Compute
Simulate or log games; fit ratings (Elo, Bradley–Terry); the counterfactual swap test, which replaces one slot and reruns the same opponents.
Fails when
You read "appears on winning teams" as "causes wins". Incineroar is on 29% of teams, so it is on many winning teams by default.
↑ average over opponents
4 · DecisionsWhat should I click?
Represent
A simultaneous-move game: payoff matrix per turn, information sets for what you cannot see.
Compute
Minimax / MCTS search, mixed strategies, regret minimization (CFR).
Fails when
You pick one "best move". Both players choose at once, so any fixed answer can be read and punished.
↑ choose under uncertainty
3 · InteractionsWhat does this pairing enable?
Represent
Graph of roles and plans. Many effects need three or more things at once (setter + redirector + field), so they are hyperedges or conditional rules, not pairs.
Compute
Graph queries, production rules, dependency walks: "if Sinistcha faints, which plans lose their redirector?"
Fails when
Pairwise "X counters Y" edges ignore the board. Rage Powder works until the target is Grass type, has Stalwart, or the redirector is gone.
↑ weight by likelihood
2 · BeliefsWhat are they running?
Represent
Probability tables: usage stats as priors, updated by team preview and what gets revealed.
Compute
Bayes' rule. Conditioning on teammates, items and player history.
Fails when
Possible gets treated as likely. This is the Stalwart Archaludon mistake below.
↑ what exists at all
1 · MechanicsWhat can happen?
Represent
The simulator's source code and a damage calculator. Rules as executable code, not prose.
Compute
Run it. Showdown resolves ability, item, field and priority order exactly.
Fails when
An LLM recalls a rule from text. It forgets edge cases (Grass types ignore Rage Powder) or invents them.
Worked example · layer 1 vs layer 2

"Archaludon beats Sinistcha's Rage Powder"

The mechanism is real: Stalwart ignores redirection, so a Stalwart Archaludon fires through Rage Powder. A graph that stores only mechanisms will flag Archaludon as a counter. Players know it almost never matters, because almost nobody runs Stalwart. That piece of tribal knowledge is a number you can look up.

So the threat is a product, and one factor is tiny:

threat ≈ [mechanism exists] × Pr(Stalwart | Archaludon) × Pr(it matters this game)
        = 1 × 0.024 × (…)
Stalwart IGNORES redirection× P = 0.024→ a weak edge, not a counter

What Archaludon actually brings: Electro Shot on 96.8% of sets, Leftovers on 84.8%, Pelipper as a teammate on about 56%. Rain lets Electro Shot fire in one turn. Against Grass/Ghost Sinistcha, Electric is resisted, so the relationship that matters is a different one entirely.

Lesson for the graph: every mechanism edge needs a weight from layer 2. Store "Stalwart ignores redirection" once as a rule, and store "Archaludon runs Stalwart 2.4%" as a dated observation with its source. The query multiplies them. Your pokemon-core-lab already has both node types (Species/Ability and Usage/Snapshot); the missing piece is the query that joins them.

Worked example · layer 2

The Volcarona spidey sense

Some players read a Volcarona at team preview and just know it is Quiver Dance, not Rage Powder support. The usage stats show why that feels like intuition: the marginals are a coin flip.

A coin flip on its own; the experienced player is conditioning on more evidence. Teammates (Rillaboom on about 63% of Volcarona teams), the item once revealed (Grassy Seed on 35%), the rest of the six, the player's own history. That is Bayes' rule applied quickly:

posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio of each cue

One catch: Smogon's usage file gives marginals per Pokémon, not whole sets. It cannot tell you "Quiver Dance given Rillaboom teammate". For that you need set-level data, such as replays or team sheets. This is the gap between the stats and the spidey sense.

Worked example · layer 4

Why the answer is a distribution

Volcarona can Quiver Dance, Rage Powder or Protect. The opponent can attack Volcarona, attack its partner, or set up. Both choose at the same time. The payoffs below are invented to show the shape of the problem; a real version would get them from the engine.

You \ ThemAttack VolcaronaAttack partnerSet up
Quiver Dance−2+1+2
Rage Powder−1+2−1
Protect+1−1−1

Press run. Both sides play thousands of rounds of regret matching: after each round, each side asks "how much better would each other option have done?" and shifts weight toward the ones it regrets not playing. The average strategy settles on a mix where neither side can gain by changing alone. That is a Nash equilibrium, and CFR is this same loop extended to every decision point in a game tree.

0 rounds

Result: you play Protect most often, Quiver Dance sometimes and Rage Powder rarely, and the opponent mixes too. Any pure "always Quiver Dance" gets read. When people say a player was "unpredictable", this mix is what they mean.

Your question

Can a graph database fix LLM reasoning about interactions?

Partly. A graph stores facts and finds them fast. It does not compute what happens when Intimidate, a Sitrus Berry, Grassy Terrain and a speed tie meet on one turn. That is the engine's job. What fixes the LLM is splitting the work so each part does what it is reliable at:

PartGood atBad atIn your lab
LLMProposing hypotheses, naming plans, explaining in human termsTracking state, exact rule order, knowing what is rareWrites the candidate: "Sinistcha + Indeedee for double redirection"
GraphFacts with sources, priors with dates, which plan depends on which pieceAny outcome that depends on order, damage or chanceSpecies, moves, Usage, Snapshot nodes; reverse dependency walk
Engine / calcGround truth for one exact situationSaying which situations to tryShowdown at the pinned revision
Search / game theoryPicking an action when the opponent is also choosingHuge trees without abstractionSmall simultaneous-move games over engine outcomes

The loop is: the LLM proposes, the graph supplies facts and priors, the engine checks, search decides, and the graph stores the result with its conditions. PokéChamp works this way: an LLM inside a minimax search.

The opposite camp is the last row alone: skip hand-built structure and learn from data at scale. Metamon and VGC-Bench go this way, and Sutton's "Bitter Lesson" argues it usually wins. A graph earns its place where you need explanations, provenance and fast updates when the rules change.

Reading path

  1. Saturday morning, layers 1–2. Open the damage calc and run three calcs you think you know. Then do Conditional probability, visually (10 min) and 3Blue1Brown on Bayes (15 min).
  2. Saturday afternoon, layer 3. Read Algorithmic Pokémon Teambuilding: strength, synergy and matchup as separate terms. Then skim the Smogon VGC cores thread and note every core that needs three pieces.
  3. Sunday morning, layer 4. Read the rock-paper-scissors part of Neller & Lanctot's CFR tutorial; it is the toy above with the code. Then Quanta on why equilibria are hard to reach.
  4. Sunday afternoon, the bots. Abstracts and figures only: PokéChamp (LLM + minimax), VGC-Bench (doubles, team diversity), Metamon (offline RL). Finish with the Bitter Lesson as the counterargument to building a graph at all.
  5. Then build one query. In pokemon-core-lab: for each mechanism edge that threatens your team, multiply by its usage weight and list the top five real threats. Check the top one in the engine.
